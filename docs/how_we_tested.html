How we tested whether surnames cost candidates votes

Every test compares candidates who stand in the same race, so place, year and turnout cancel out. This page traces the data from raw files to results, shows which rules were fixed before any results were seen, and shows the two follow-up tests that explained the surprises.

Follow-up tests

The penalty depends on who the voters are

Two follow-ups asked whether a name does better where more residents share its background. The co-ethnic test used Hispanic, Black and Asian population shares. The ancestry-bloc test used Census ancestry for German, Nordic, French, Italian and Eastern European names. Both point the same way.

Keeping it honest

Which rules were fixed before seeing results

  1. before resultsPredictions H1 and H2Written in the spec: non-white and foreign names carry a large penalty (H1); foreign but white-coded names carry little or none (H2).
  2. before resultsForeign coding rule and its sensitivity versionAnything outside British/Irish counts as foreign; the sensitivity rule also treats German, French and Nordic as not foreign.
  3. before resultsOverperformance and swing designsAdded after spotting the party-and-district problem, before any regression ran.
  4. before resultsCheckpoint and Middle Eastern / Muslim-origin flagCell counts and a hand-reviewed list of 242 surnames, done without vote data.
  5. after main resultsFour-group comparison and robustness checksContest-clustered errors, a 0/1 foreign score, open seats only, and multi-seat races.
  6. after main resultsCo-ethnic test and ancestry-bloc testExploratory. Each one's rules were written down before its demographic or ancestry data was loaded.
Where it lands

What the tests show

  • 1On average, candidates in all four name groups do within about a point of a white Anglo opponent in the same race. A large average penalty is ruled out.
  • 2That average hides a pattern: names lose votes where few residents share their background and gain where many do. This holds for Hispanic, Black and Asian surnames and for white ethnic surnames.
  • 3Garcia-type names show no average penalty because they often run where their group is large. Hansen-type names show one because matching-ancestry blocs are usually small.
  • 4H1 holds only in less diverse places. H2 fails in nonpartisan local races, as in the Swiss study.

These are within-race associations, not proof of what voters intend. Candidate quality, money and decisions about where to run can still differ by name.

Surname scores measure what a name signals on paper, not how voters hear it. About 9% of names, mostly rare ones, could not be scored. The place-based tests cover local races only.
