Surnames lose votes where few voters share them, and gain where many do

Local elections. Points of vote share compared with a white, Anglo-named opponent in the same race.

In local elections, a surname seems to cost votes only where few residents share it. A candidate with a Black-coded surname got about 4 points less than a white, Anglo-named opponent in the same race where almost no residents are Black, but about 3 points more where half are. Asian and Hispanic surnames follow the same pattern, and so do German surnames when measured against how many residents report German ancestry. This explains why the overall averages looked flat. Many Hispanic and Black candidates run where their group makes up a large share of voters, so the penalty in some places and the boost in others cancel out. German, Italian or Scandinavian-origin candidates rarely run where their ancestry group is that large, so their names show a small penalty on average. These are associations rather than proof of why voters choose as they do, and the dashed ends of each line are projections beyond where most candidates actually ran.
