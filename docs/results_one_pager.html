Do foreign-sounding surnames cost candidates votes?

Not on average. But names lose votes where few voters share them and gain votes where many do.

Finding 1

Against an opponent in the same race, every group does about the same

Finding 2

The average hides who the voters are

In local races, names do worse where few residents share the candidate's background and better where many do. That holds for Black, Asian and Hispanic surnames, and for white ethnic surnames like German ones.

This explains the flat averages. Hispanic and Black candidates often run where their group is a large share of voters, so the penalty and the boost cancel out. German, Italian or Scandinavian matching blocs are usually small, so those names show a penalty instead: about −1.7 points in nonpartisan local races.

How the hypotheses held up

H1 Names that are both non-white and foreign carry a large penalty. Partly Only in less diverse places, where the penalty is about 2 points.
H2 Foreign but white-coded names carry little or no penalty. Not supported They lose 1–2 points in nonpartisan local races.
EXTRA Middle Eastern / Muslim-origin surnames. Inconclusive Only 179 candidates: +3.3 points, with a range of −2.9 to +9.4.

These are associations within races, not proof of what voters intend. Candidates with different names may also differ in money, experience or where they choose to run. Surname scores measure what a name signals on paper, not how voters hear it. The place-based findings come from local races and were tested after the main results, with their rules written down first.
